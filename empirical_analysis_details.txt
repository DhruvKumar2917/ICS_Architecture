EMPIRICAL EVALUATION OF THE DERIVED AUTHORIZATION ATTACK SURFACE GRAPH (AASG)
================================================================================

Suggested Empirical Analysis for the Derived AASG
Section VI. Empirical Evaluation of the Derived AASG

The empirical analysis evaluates the authorization-induced attack surface exposed by the 
derived Authorization Attack Surface Graph (AASG). Unlike vulnerability-centric evaluation, 
this analysis does not assume software exploitation or device compromise through known 
CVEs. Instead, it quantifies how legitimate authorization decisions and architecture-level 
trust relationships enable adversarial reachability from an externally accessible role to a 
high-impact cyber-physical asset. 

In the reference wind-farm architecture, the externally accessible role is VendorMaint, 
and the high-impact asset is the PLC, since the PLC directly affects control logic and 
physical process behavior. The AASG contains one explicit authorization edge from 
VendorMaint in Zone1 to OEM_SCADA_Server in Zone2, followed by connection edges from 
OEM_SCADA_Server to HMI_Master and PLC in Zone3. This structure shows that only the 
initial transition is explicitly policy-enforced, while subsequent reachability is 
enabled by architectural communication paths and implicit trust assumptions.

Formally, the derived AASG contains the following vertices:
  V = {VendorMaint, OEM_SCADA_Server, HMI_Master, PLC}

and the following directed edges:
  E = {e1, e2, e3, e4}

where e1 is the explicit authorization edge:
  VendorMaint -> OEM_SCADA_Server

and e2, e3, e4 are connection or trust-based edges:
  OEM_SCADA_Server -> HMI_Master
  OEM_SCADA_Server -> PLC
  HMI_Master -> PLC

The AASG therefore reveals that a single legitimate remote-maintenance authorization 
decision can induce multiple downstream paths to the PLC. This is significant because the 
downstream paths are not created by vulnerabilities; they arise from the composition of 
valid access permissions, zone-crossing communication, and protocol-level trust.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A. Policy-Induced Attack Path Enumeration
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
From the AASG, two policy-induced paths from VendorMaint to the high-impact asset PLC 
are identified:

  pi_1 = <VendorMaint, OEM_SCADA_Server, HMI_Master, PLC>
  pi_2 = <VendorMaint, OEM_SCADA_Server, PLC>

- Path pi_1 represents a multi-hop progression in which the vendor-maintenance role 
  first reaches the OEM SCADA server, then reaches the HMI Master, and finally reaches 
  the PLC through the field-link/control-protocol path.
- Path pi_2 represents a shorter direct progression from the OEM SCADA server to the 
  PLC through the PCN industrial communication path.

These two paths demonstrate that the same initial authorization decision creates more 
than one feasible route to the high-impact asset.

Path Summary Table:
+------+-------------------------------------------------------+--------------+----------------+-------------------------------+
| Path | Sequence                                              | No. of Edges | Terminal Asset | Interpretation                |
+------+-------------------------------------------------------+--------------+----------------+-------------------------------+
| pi_1 | VendorMaint -> OEM SCADA Server -> HMI Master -> PLC  | 3            | PLC            | Indirect path through HMI     |
+------+-------------------------------------------------------+--------------+----------------+-------------------------------+
| pi_2 | VendorMaint -> OEM SCADA Server -> PLC                | 2            | PLC            | Direct SCADA-to-PLC path      |
+------+-------------------------------------------------------+--------------+----------------+-------------------------------+

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
B. Enforcement and Trust Decomposition
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Each attack path is decomposed into two categories of transitions:
1. Policy-enforced transitions, which are guarded by explicit authorization decisions.
2. Assumption-based transitions, which are enabled by architectural reachability, 
   protocol semantics, or implicit zone-level trust.

For path pi_1:
  - VendorMaint -> OEM_SCADA_Server (Policy-enforced)
  - OEM_SCADA_Server -> HMI_Master (Assumption-based)
  - HMI_Master -> PLC (Assumption-based)

For path pi_2:
  - VendorMaint -> OEM_SCADA_Server (Policy-enforced)
  - OEM_SCADA_Server -> PLC (Assumption-based)

This decomposition shows that the enforcement boundary is concentrated at the first hop. 
After the vendor role reaches the OEM SCADA server, subsequent movement toward the PLC 
is governed primarily by architectural trust and industrial communication assumptions 
rather than additional authorization checks. This finding is central to the paper’s argument: 
access-control policies may be correct locally but unsafe when composed with 
downstream trust relationships.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
C. Authorization-Centric Metric Results
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The following three metrics quantify the empirical security impact of the derived AASG:

1. Authorization Amplification Factor (AAF)
   The Authorization Amplification Factor, denoted as AAF(p), measures how many 
   high-impact actions become reachable per explicit authorization enforcement point.

             |A_HI(p)|
   AAF(p) = -----------
            max(1, |E_policy(p)|)

   where A_HI(p) is the set of high-impact actions reachable along path p, and 
   E_policy(p) is the set of explicit policy-enforced transitions along the path.

   For both pi_1 and pi_2, there is only one explicit policy-enforced transition:
     |E_policy(pi_1)| = 1
     |E_policy(pi_2)| = 1

   For pi_1, the high-impact reachable action is associated with issuing command 
   messages toward the PLC through the HMI path. In the ATG, this corresponds to 
   T0862: Command Message. 
   For pi_2, the high-impact reachable action is associated with direct modification 
   or manipulation of PLC logic, corresponding to T0831: Modify Control Logic.

   Thus:
     AAF(pi_1) = 1/1 = 1
     AAF(pi_2) = 1/1 = 1

   Although the numerical value is 1 for each individual path, the important empirical 
   observation is that one authorization decision produces multiple distinct high-impact 
   paths. Therefore, at the role level:

                               |A_HI(pi_1) U A_HI(pi_2)|
     AAF_role(VendorMaint) = ----------------------------
                                      |E_policy|

     AAF_role(VendorMaint) = 2/1 = 2

   This means that one explicit authorization decision associated with the VendorMaint role 
   enables two high-impact adversarial capabilities: command-message execution and 
   control-logic modification. This role-level interpretation better demonstrates 
   authorization amplification than analyzing each path in isolation.

2. Trust Expansion Length (TEL)
   Trust Expansion Length, denoted as TEL(p), measures how far an attacker can progress 
   after the last explicit policy-enforcement point.

   For pi_1:
     TEL(pi_1) = 2
     (because two assumption-based transitions occur after the initial policy-enforced hop:
      OEM_SCADA_Server -> HMI_Master, and HMI_Master -> PLC)

   For pi_2:
     TEL(pi_2) = 1
     (because one assumption-based transition occurs after the initial policy-enforced hop:
      OEM_SCADA_Server -> PLC)

   The average trust expansion length is therefore:
     TEL_avg = (2 + 1) / 2 = 1.5

   The maximum trust expansion length is:
     TEL_max = 2

   This result shows that once the initial authorization boundary is crossed, trust 
   propagates one to two additional hops toward the PLC without additional authorization 
   validation. In CPS environments, this is security-relevant because downstream assets 
   may directly affect physical processes even if the initial access was intended only for 
   remote maintenance.

3. Cross-Zone Impact Span
   Cross-Zone Impact Span measures the number of distinct security zones traversed by 
   a policy-induced path.

   Both paths traverse:
     Zone1 -> Zone2 -> Zone3

   where:
     - Zone1 represents the Vendor Control Room.
     - Zone2 represents the OEM Control Center.
     - Zone3 represents the Wind Turbine Domain.

   Therefore:
     Span(pi_1) = 3
     Span(pi_2) = 3

   This indicates that a single authorization decision originating in the vendor domain can 
   propagate across three distinct trust zones and terminate in the operational control 
   domain. The result demonstrates a containment weakness: the logical segmentation into 
   zones does not, by itself, prevent authorization-induced reachability from crossing 
   administrative and operational boundaries.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
E. Key Empirical Findings
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The empirical results provide four important findings:

1. Multiple Policy-Induced Paths:
   The derived AASG exposes multiple policy-induced paths from an externally 
   accessible role to a high-impact PLC. This shows that adversarial reachability 
   can emerge as a consequence of legitimate authorization and architecture 
   composition, even without assuming exploitable software vulnerabilities.

2. Concentration at Boundaries:
   The enforcement analysis shows that authorization checks are heavily concentrated 
   at the initial boundary between Zone1 and Zone2. After the VendorMaint role 
   gains access to the OEM_SCADA_Server, subsequent movement to the HMI_Master 
   and the PLC is governed by trust-based industrial communication paths. This 
   highlights a critical gap between access approval at the remote boundary and 
   continued enforcement inside the operational environment.

3. Authorization Amplification:
   The metrics show measurable authorization amplification. At the role level, 
   a single authorization decision enables two distinct high-impact action classes 
   (command-message execution and control-logic modification). This supports the 
   paper's main claim that access-control configurations can amplify adversarial 
   capability when composed with downstream architectural trust assumptions.

4. Containment Weakness of Segmentation:
   Both paths traverse three zones, demonstrating that network or architectural 
   segmentation alone does not guarantee containment. The AASG reveals that 
   cross-zone reachability is not merely a topology issue; it is a policy-composition 
   issue involving roles, permissions, conduits, and implicit trust relationships.
